Unit 1

Linear Equations & Graphs★ midterm

Barnett §1.1–1.2 · solving linear equations/inequalities, slope, modeling
Foundations
  • Solve a linear equation: distribute, combine like terms, undo with inverse operations to isolate the variable.
  • Inequalities solve the same way — but flip the sign when you multiply or divide by a negative.
  • Line forms: y = mx + b · point-slope y − y₁ = m(x − x₁) · standard Ax + By = C.
  • Intercepts: x-int set y = 0; y-int set x = 0.

Nothing new for you here — slope-intercept, point-slope, intercepts. The only course flavor is applied linear models: cost/revenue, break-even, and straight-line depreciation. Camila needs to translate a word problem into y = mx + b and interpret the slope as a rate.

slope m = (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁) · y = mx + b · break-even: Cost = Revenue
Teaching Camila

The recurring stumble is sign and "which is the rate." In Cost = 50 + 4x, push her to say out loud: "$50 fixed, $4 per unit." Break-even is just setting two lines equal — frame it as "where the two lines cross," which she can also see graphically.

Unit 2

Matrices & Gauss-Jordan★ midterm

Barnett §4.1–4.6 · systems, matrix operations, Gauss-Jordan elimination, inverses
Foundations
  • A matrix is an array of rows × columns; entry aᵢⱼ sits in row i, column j.
  • Pre-matrix system solving: substitution (solve one equation for a variable, sub into the other) or elimination (scale a row and add to cancel a variable).
  • Matrix multiply = row · column dot products; defined only when inner dimensions match.
  • 2×2: det = ad − bc; the identity I has 1s on the diagonal, 0s elsewhere.

You know matrix algebra. The course-specific procedure is Gauss-Jordan by hand to reduced row-echelon form (RREF), then reading the solution straight off. The three legal row operations: swap two rows, multiply a row by a nonzero constant, add a multiple of one row to another. The goal is a leading 1 in each pivot column with 0s above and below it.

Also tested: matrix add/subtract (entry-by-entry, same dimensions), scalar multiply, and matrix multiplication (row · column dot products; inner dimensions must match, product takes the outer dimensions). The §4.6 piece is solving AX = B as X = A⁻¹B; for 2×2, A⁻¹ = (1/det) d−b−ca with det = ad − bc.

Teaching Camila

Two classic errors: she'll try to multiply matrices entry-by-entry (it's row-by-column), and she'll lose signs during row operations. Have her write the operation she's doing next to each new row — the bookkeeping is most of the battle. Reading RREF is the payoff: a row like [0 1 | −2] literally says "y = −2."

Unit 3

Linear Programming★ midterm

Barnett §5.1–5.3 (graphical) and §6.1–6.2 (simplex)
Foundations
  • Graph a line fast from its two intercepts; connect them.
  • A linear inequality is a half-plane — boundary solid for ≤/≥, dashed for </>.
  • Test a point (usually the origin) to decide which side to shade.
  • A corner point = the solution of the two boundary lines solved as a system (Unit 2).

Two methods for the same kind of problem. Graphical (two variables): graph each constraint as a half-plane, find the feasible region (the intersection), then evaluate the objective at every corner point — the optimum always sits at a vertex. Simplex (any number of variables): an algorithmic version of the same idea, walking corner to corner via a tableau.

Worked example · graphical

Maximize P = 3x + 4y subject to x + y ≤ 4, x + 3y ≤ 6, x, y ≥ 0.

Corners of the feasible region: (0,0), (4,0), (0,2), and the intersection of the two constraint lines, (3,1). Evaluate P:

  • (0,0) → 0   ·   (4,0) → 12   ·   (0,2) → 8   ·   (3,1) → 13 ✓ max
Worked example · simplex (same problem)

Add slack variables to make constraints into equations: x + y + s₁ = 4, x + 3y + s₂ = 6, and write the objective row as P − 3x − 4y = 0.

Pivot rule: most-negative entry in the objective row picks the column (−4 → y). The ratio test (RHS ÷ positive column entry, smallest wins) picks the row: 4/1 = 4 vs 6/3 = 2, so row 2. Pivot on the 3, clear the column. Repeat: next pivot is the x-column, ratio test gives row 1. After that the objective row has no negatives left — optimal.

Result: P = 13 at x = 3, y = 1 — same answer the graph gave, which is the reassuring check.

Teaching Camila

For graphical, the make-or-break is plotting constraints correctly and finding the corner coordinates (often by solving two equations simultaneously — ties back to Unit 2). For "which side to shade," test the origin: if it satisfies the inequality, shade toward it. For simplex, she doesn't need the theory — she needs the loop: pick column (most negative), pick row (ratio test), pivot, repeat until no negatives. Doing two full problems by hand makes it muscle memory; that's what the exam rewards.

Unit 4

Sets & Counting

Barnett §7.2–7.4 · sets, Venn diagrams, multiplication principle, permutations & combinations
Foundations
  • Set notation: ∈ in · ⊆ subset · ∪ or · ∩ and · A′ not · ∅ empty · U universal.
  • |A| = cardinality (count of elements).
  • Factorial: n! = n·(n−1)···2·1, with 0! = 1.
  • Multiplication principle: independent choices multiply (m ways, then n ways → m·n).

Set basics (union, intersection, complement, cardinality) plus counting. The one formula students forget is inclusion-exclusion; the one distinction they blur is permutations vs combinations.

|A ∪ B| = |A| + |B| − |A ∩ B| · P(n,k) = n!/(n−k)! · C(n,k) = n!/[k!(n−k)!]

Permutations count ordered arrangements; combinations count unordered selections. The multiplication principle underlies both: independent choices multiply. A set of n elements has 2ⁿ subsets.

Teaching Camila

Give her one question to ask every counting problem: "Does order matter?" A committee of 3 (order doesn't matter) → combination; a president-VP-treasurer slate (order matters) → permutation. For Venn problems, fill in the intersection first, then work outward so regions aren't double-counted — that's exactly what inclusion-exclusion is doing algebraically.

Unit 5

Probability

Barnett §8.1–8.3 · sample spaces, addition rule, conditional probability, independence
Foundations
  • P(event) = favorable / total for equally likely outcomes, and 0 ≤ P ≤ 1.
  • Sample space = the set of all possible outcomes.
  • Complement: P(not A) = 1 − P(A).
  • Fluency moving between fractions, decimals, and percents.

Standard discrete probability. The three load-bearing rules:

P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B) · P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B) · independent: P(A ∩ B) = P(A)·P(B)

The course leans on tree diagrams for multi-stage experiments and conditional probability. Odds-to-probability shows up too: odds a:b in favor means probability a/(a+b).

Worked example · tree / conditional

Machine A makes 60% of parts (2% defective); Machine B makes 40% (5% defective). Probability a random part is defective?

P(def) = 0.60·0.02 + 0.40·0.05 = 0.012 + 0.020 = 0.032

And if a part is defective, the probability it came from A: P(A | def) = 0.012 / 0.032 = 0.375.

Teaching Camila

The big trap is "and" vs "or": "or" adds (and subtracts the overlap), "and" multiplies (for independent events). When in doubt, draw the tree — branch probabilities multiply along a path, and you add across the paths that satisfy the event. Conditional probability is just "shrink the world to B, then ask about A."

Unit 6

Expected Value

Barnett §8.5 · expected value of a random variable
Foundations
  • Weighted average: multiply each value by its weight, then add the products.
  • Σ (sigma) means "sum all the terms."
  • The weights here are probabilities — they must cover all outcomes and sum to 1.

A weighted average of outcomes by their probabilities. Conceptually trivial for you; the application framing (is a game/bet fair?) is the point.

E(X) = Σ xᵢ · P(xᵢ) · fair game ⇔ E(X) = 0
Worked example

Win $10 with probability 0.2, lose $3 with probability 0.8:

E(X) = (0.2)(10) + (0.8)(−3) = 2 − 2.4 = −$0.40 per play — a losing game in the long run.

Teaching Camila

Two reminders: losses are negative values (sign errors are the #1 mistake), and the probabilities must cover every outcome and sum to 1. Frame the answer as "average per play over many plays," which makes a negative expected value feel concrete.

Unit 7

Markov Chains

Barnett §9.1–9.2 · transition matrices, state vectors, steady state
Foundations
  • Probability vector: nonnegative entries that sum to 1.
  • Row vector × matrix: [a b]·P combines the row entries with each column — a 1×n times n×n gives 1×n.
  • Matrix power Pⁿ = P multiplied by itself n times (n-step transitions).

This is the topic most likely to feel unfamiliar even to an engineer. A transition matrix P has rows that each sum to 1 (the probabilities of moving from one state to all states). Multiply a state vector by P to step forward: Sₙ₊₁ = Sₙ·P. A regular chain (some power of P has all-positive entries) converges to a unique steady-state vector π, regardless of where you start.

Finding π is the key technique: solve π·P = π together with "entries sum to 1."

Worked example · steady state

P = 0.80.20.30.7. Let π = [a, 1−a]. The first component of π·P = π gives:

0.8a + 0.3(1−a) = a → 0.5a + 0.3 = a → a = 0.6

So π = [0.6, 0.4]. (Check: [0.6, 0.4]·P = [0.6, 0.4] ✓.)

Teaching Camila

Anchor it with a story — weather (sunny/rainy), or customers switching between two brands. "Where does it settle long-term, no matter today's weather?" is the steady state. Mechanically, the two things she must not forget: row vector times matrix (state on the left), and rows of P sum to 1. The steady-state solve is just a small system of equations — back to Unit 2 again.

Unit 8

Bernoulli & Binomial

Barnett §10.4 · Bernoulli trials, the binomial distribution
Foundations
  • Combinations C(n,k) = ways to choose k of n, order ignored (Unit 4).
  • Exponents: pᵏ = p multiplied k times; p⁰ = 1.
  • Independent events multiply; complementary probabilities p and 1−p sum to 1.

A Bernoulli trial is a single success/failure experiment with fixed probability p. The binomial distribution counts successes over n independent trials:

P(X = k) = C(n,k) · pᵏ · (1−p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ · mean = n·p

The three ingredients are the count of arrangements C(n,k) (ties back to Unit 4), the successes pᵏ, and the failures (1−p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ. For "at least one," it's almost always faster to use the complement: 1 − P(none).

Worked example

Five fair coin flips, probability of exactly 2 heads:

C(5,2)·(0.5)²·(0.5)³ = 10 · (1/32) = 0.3125

Teaching Camila

Make sure she sees the three pieces of the formula and why each is there — the C(n,k) is "how many ways the k successes can be placed among n trials." For "at least one," steer her to the complement trick before she tries to add up many cases. The expected number of successes is just np.
