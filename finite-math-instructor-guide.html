Finite Math, taught from the ground upA deeper guide for helping Camila — foundations, full worked examples, and a coaching plan for each topic

This version is built for the situation where the student is genuinely behind. Each topic gives you the foundations in shorthand, the method explained slowly with the "why," several fully-worked examples from easy to harder, the specific mistakes students make, and a concrete plan for teaching it to someone who's struggling. Remember the exam reality: Desmos and Excel are banned, so every method here must be done by hand — that's what you're building toward.

How to use this with a student who's behind

A few principles that make the difference when someone is lost, woven through every "how to teach it" box below:

  1. Concrete before abstract. Always start with real numbers and a real situation, then generalize. Never open with the formula.
  2. One technique at a time. Don't mix topics in a session. Master the move, then add the next.
  3. Watch, then guided, then solo. You work one example fully; you work the next together; she works the third while you only nudge. Three reps beats one lecture.
  4. Make her narrate. "Tell me what you're about to do and why." Saying it out loud surfaces the gap better than watching her write.
  5. Check every answer by substitution. It catches errors and — more importantly — it builds her confidence that she can verify her own work.
  6. Front-load the midterm units. Units 1–3 (★) carry the first exam and the heaviest weight. Get those solid first; the later units lean on the same skills.

After teaching a unit, send her to that unit in the Practice Studio — the worked solutions there reinforce exactly what you covered.

Unit 1

Linear Equations & Graphs★ midterm

Barnett §1.1–1.2 · solving linear equations/inequalities, slope, modeling
Foundations
  • Solve a linear equation: distribute, combine like terms, undo with inverse operations to isolate the variable.
  • Inequalities solve the same way — but flip the sign when you multiply or divide by a negative.
  • Line forms: y = mx + b · point-slope y − y₁ = m(x − x₁) · standard Ax + By = C.
  • Intercepts: x-int set y = 0; y-int set x = 0.

The method

Solving. The goal is always to get the variable alone. Work in reverse order of operations: undo addition/subtraction first, then multiplication/division. If there are parentheses, distribute; if the variable appears on both sides, collect it on one side. Inequalities follow the identical steps, with the one extra rule that multiplying or dividing both sides by a negative reverses the inequality symbol.

Lines. Slope m = (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁) is the rate of change — how much y moves per one-unit step in x. In y = mx + b, the slope m is that rate and b is the starting value (the y-intercept). To graph, plot b on the y-axis, then use the slope as rise-over-run to step to the next point.

Modeling. Most word problems here are a line in disguise: find the fixed amount (that's b) and the per-unit rate (that's m). Break-even means setting two expressions equal (cost = revenue). Straight-line depreciation subtracts a fixed amount each year from a starting value.

Worked examples

Example 1 · multi-step equation

Solve 2(3x − 4) = 4x + 10.

Distribute the 2: 6x − 8 = 4x + 10

Collect x on the left, numbers on the right: 6x − 4x = 10 + 8 → 2x = 18

Divide by 2: x = 9. Check: 2(27−4) = 46 and 4(9)+10 = 46 ✓

Example 2 · build a line from two points

Find the equation of the line through (−1, 5) and (2, −1).

Slope: m = (−1 − 5)/(2 − (−1)) = −6/3 = −2

Point-slope with (2, −1): y + 1 = −2(x − 2) → y = −2x + 3

Check the other point (−1, 5): −2(−1)+3 = 5 ✓

Example 3 · break-even (application)

A vendor pays $300 to rent a booth plus $2 per item to make, and sells each item for $5. How many items to break even?

Cost C = 300 + 2x; Revenue R = 5x

Set equal: 300 + 2x = 5x → 300 = 3x → x = 100 items

Example 4 · inequality with a sign flip

Solve −3x + 7 ≥ 1.

Subtract 7: −3x ≥ −6

Divide by −3 — flip the sign: x ≤ 2

Where Camila will trip
  • Distributing to only the first term: 2(3x − 4) becomes 6x − 8, not 6x − 4.
  • Sign errors when moving a term across the equals sign (it changes sign).
  • Forgetting to flip the inequality when dividing by a negative.
  • In word problems, mixing up which number is the rate (slope) and which is the fixed start (intercept).
How to teach it

Open every equation with one question: "What's being done to x?" — then undo it in reverse. Have her say the steps aloud before writing. For lines, anchor "slope = rise over run" with a physical image (stairs or a ramp), and always plug the answer back in to check. For word problems, before any algebra, have her point to the fixed part and the per-item part out loud; the equation falls out once those are labeled. This unit is mostly review for her — use it to rebuild confidence and the substitution-checking habit she'll need everywhere else.

Unit 2

Matrices & Gauss-Jordan★ midterm

Barnett §4.1–4.6 · systems, matrix operations, Gauss-Jordan elimination, inverses
Foundations
  • A matrix is an array of rows × columns; entry aᵢⱼ sits in row i, column j.
  • Pre-matrix system solving: substitution (solve one equation for a variable, sub into the other) or elimination (scale a row and add to cancel a variable).
  • Matrix multiply = row · column dot products; defined only when inner dimensions match.
  • 2×2: det = ad − bc; the identity I has 1s on the diagonal, 0s elsewhere.

The method

Start with plain systems. Before matrices, two equations in two unknowns are solved by substitution (solve one equation for a variable, plug into the other) or elimination (scale equations so a variable cancels when you add them). Gauss-Jordan is just elimination, organized.

The augmented matrix. Write the coefficients in a grid and the constants in a last column (the bar). Each row is one equation. There are exactly three legal moves: swap two rows, multiply a row by a nonzero number, or add a multiple of one row to another.

The goal (RREF). Drive the left side to the identity: a leading 1 in each pivot position, with 0s above and below it. The strategy is a staircase — get a 1 in the top-left, clear the rest of that column, move down-right to the next pivot, repeat, then clear upward. When the left is the identity, the constants column is the answer.

Matrix arithmetic. Add/subtract entrywise (same size only). Scalar multiply hits every entry. Multiplication is row-times-column dot products; the inner dimensions must match and order matters (AB ≠ BA). For a 2×2, the inverse is A⁻¹ = (1/det) d−b−ca, and a system AX = B solves as X = A⁻¹B.

Worked examples

Example 1 · elimination, no matrix yet

Solve 2x + 3y = 12 and x − y = 1.

From the second equation, x = y + 1. Substitute: 2(y+1) + 3y = 12

2y + 2 + 3y = 12 → 5y = 10 → y = 2, so x = 3

Solution (3, 2). Check: 2(3)+3(2)=12 ✓, 3−2=1 ✓

  • Multiplying matrices entry-by-entry instead of row-by-column — the single most common error.
  • Arithmetic and sign slips inside a row operation (this is where 3×3 problems go wrong).

  • Trying to multiply matrices whose inner dimensions don't match.
  • Losing track of which row operation she just did.
How to teach it

Teach substitution and elimination first, until she can solve a 2×2 system without hesitation — then introduce the matrix as "the same elimination, written more neatly." Make her write the row operation next to every new row, every single time; that bookkeeping habit is most of the battle. Drill one mantra at each pivot: "a 1 in the spot, 0s in the rest of the column." For multiplication, have her physically trace the row with one finger and the column with another so the motion becomes automatic. When the 3×3 looks intimidating, reassure her it's the exact same three moves, just with more rows — and always finish by checking the solution in the original equations.

Unit 3

Linear Programming★ midterm

Barnett §5.1–5.3 (graphical) and §6.1–6.2 (simplex)
Foundations
  • Graph a line fast from its two intercepts; connect them.
  • A linear inequality is a half-plane — boundary solid for ≤/≥, dashed for </>.
  • Test a point (usually the origin) to decide which side to shade.
  • A corner point = the solution of the two boundary lines solved as a system (Unit 2).

The method — graphical (two variables)

The whole procedure is seven steps: (1) translate the words into an objective function and constraints; (2) graph each constraint as a line; (3) shade the correct side of each (test the origin); (4) the feasible region is where all the shadings overlap; (5) find every corner point by solving the lines that cross there; (6) plug each corner into the objective; (7) pick the biggest (max) or smallest (min). It works because a linear objective reaches its extreme value at a vertex — never in the middle.

The method — simplex (more than two variables)

When you can't graph, simplex hops from corner to corner systematically. Set up by turning each ≤ constraint into an equation with a slack variable, and write the objective as a bottom row. Then loop: pick the pivot column (most negative entry in the objective row), pick the pivot row (smallest nonnegative ratio of RHS ÷ that column's entry), pivot (make the pivot a 1 and clear its column), and repeat until the objective row has no negatives left.

Worked examples

Example 1 · graphical maximization

Maximize P = 3x + 4y subject to x + y ≤ 4, x + 3y ≤ 6, x, y ≥ 0.

Corner points: (0,0); on the x-axis x+y=4 → (4,0); on the y-axis x+3y=6 → (0,2); and where the two lines cross — subtract them: 2y = 2 → y=1, x=3 → (3,1).

Evaluate P at each: (0,0)→0, (4,0)→12, (0,2)→8, (3,1)→13.

Maximum is P = 13 at (3, 1).

Example 2 · graphical minimization (≥ constraints)

Minimize C = 2x + 3y subject to x + y ≥ 8, 2x + y ≥ 10, x, y ≥ 0.

Corner points of this region: (0,10) on the y-axis; where the two lines cross — subtract: x = 2, y = 6 → (2,6); and (8,0) on the x-axis.

Evaluate C: (0,10)→30, (2,6)→22, (8,0)→16.

Minimum is C = 16 at (8, 0). (With "≥" the region opens upward, but the minimum still sits at a corner.)

Where Camila will trip
  • Shading the wrong side — skipping the origin test, or being thrown when the origin sits on the line.
  • Reading a corner's coordinates off the graph instead of solving the two lines exactly.
  • Forgetting to test every corner (the max often hides at the least obvious one).
  • Mixing up maximize vs minimize.
  • Simplex: not choosing the most-negative column, or doing the ratio test with negative entries, or an arithmetic slip in the pivot.
How to teach it

Make the graphical method visual and physical: graph big, color in the feasible region, literally circle each corner, and build a little two-column table of corner → objective value. That table is the anti-anxiety device — it turns a scary problem into "just fill in the rows." Drill the origin test as a simple yes/no. For simplex, don't teach the theory; teach the four-step loop as a recipe she repeats — pick column, pick row, pivot, check for negatives. Work two full problems with her, then have her narrate the steps with no help. The confidence-builder: solve one two-variable problem both ways and watch the answers match.

Unit 4

Sets & Counting

Barnett §7.2–7.4 · sets, Venn diagrams, multiplication principle, permutations & combinations
Foundations
  • Set notation: ∈ in · ⊆ subset · ∪ or · ∩ and · A′ not · ∅ empty · U universal.
  • |A| = cardinality (count of elements).
  • Factorial: n! = n·(n−1)···2·1, with 0! = 1.
  • Multiplication principle: independent choices multiply (m ways, then n ways → m·n).

The method

Sets and unions. Union (∪, "or"), intersection (∩, "and"), and complement (A′, "not"). To count a union without double-counting the overlap, use inclusion-exclusion: |A ∪ B| = |A| + |B| − |A ∩ B|.

The multiplication principle is the engine of counting: if one choice has m options and a following independent choice has n options, there are m·n combined outcomes.

Permutations vs combinations — the entire unit hinges on this one distinction. A permutation counts ordered arrangements: P(n,k) = n!/(n−k)!. A combination counts unordered selections: C(n,k) = n!/[k!(n−k)!]. The deciding question is always: does the order matter?

|A ∪ B| = |A| + |B| − |A ∩ B| · P(n,k) = n!/(n−k)! · C(n,k) = n!/[k!(n−k)!]

Worked examples

Example 1 · inclusion-exclusion in context

In a class of 30, 18 take Spanish, 15 take French, and 7 take both. How many take at least one language? How many take neither?

At least one: |S ∪ F| = 18 + 15 − 7 = 26

Neither: 30 − 26 = 4

Example 2 · permutation (order matters)

How many ways to award gold, silver, and bronze among 8 runners?

Order matters (gold ≠ bronze): P(8,3) = 8·7·6 = 336

Example 3 · combination (order doesn't matter)

How many different 5-card hands can be dealt from a 52-card deck?

A hand is unordered: C(52,5) = (52·51·50·49·48)/(5·4·3·2·1) = 2,598,960

Example 4 · multiplication principle + a combination

A pizza is built from 1 of 3 crusts, 1 of 4 sauces, and any 2 of 6 toppings. How many possible pizzas?

Multiply the independent choices: 3 × 4 × C(6,2) = 3 × 4 × 15 = 180

Where Camila will trip
  • Using a permutation when it should be a combination (or vice versa) — almost always an order-question mistake.
  • Forgetting to subtract the intersection in a union (double-counting the overlap).
  • Confusing "and" (multiply within one outcome) with separate cases (add the cases).
  • Mis-canceling factorials when simplifying C(n,k).
How to teach it

Hand her one question to ask every counting problem: "If I rearrange the chosen items, is it a different outcome?" Yes → permutation; No → combination. Drill it with five quick scenarios (a committee, a podium, a lock code, a lottery, a starting lineup) until the sort is instant. For Venn problems, always fill the center first (the intersection) and work outward — that's inclusion-exclusion made visual. For multi-step counts, have her draw a slot for each choice, write the number of options in each slot, and multiply. And to make a factorial click, have her list out all 6 arrangements of three items by hand so 3! = 6 stops being abstract.

Unit 5

Probability

Barnett §8.1–8.3 · sample spaces, addition rule, conditional probability, independence
Foundations
  • P(event) = favorable / total for equally likely outcomes, and 0 ≤ P ≤ 1.
  • Sample space = the set of all possible outcomes.
  • Complement: P(not A) = 1 − P(A).
  • Fluency moving between fractions, decimals, and percents.

The method

Single events use favorable-over-total. The complement rule (1 − P(A)) is a workhorse, especially for "at least one." The addition rule handles "or": P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B) — subtract the overlap so it isn't counted twice (for mutually exclusive events there's no overlap). "And" multiplies: independent events give P(A)·P(B); dependent events need conditional probability, P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B)/P(B) — read as "given B happened, restrict the world to B." Multi-stage problems are cleanest as a tree: multiply along each branch, add across the paths that satisfy the event.

P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B) · P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B) · independent: P(A ∩ B) = P(A)·P(B)

Worked examples

Example 1 · addition rule with overlap

Draw one card from 52. Find P(king or heart).

P(K) = 4/52, P(heart) = 13/52, overlap (king of hearts) = 1/52

P(K ∪ heart) = (4 + 13 − 1)/52 = 16/52 = 4/13

Example 2 · "at least one" via the complement

Roll a die three times. Find P(at least one 6).

Easier to find the opposite: P(no 6) = (5/6)³ = 125/216

P(at least one 6) = 1 − 125/216 = 91/216 ≈ 0.421

Example 3 · dependent draws (a tree)

A bag has 3 red and 2 blue marbles. Draw 2 without replacing. Find P(both red).

First red: 3/5. Now 2 red of 4 remain, so second red: 2/4.

Multiply along the branch: (3/5)(2/4) = 6/20 = 3/10

Example 4 · conditional probability (a tree forward and back)

Machine A makes 60% of parts (2% defective); Machine B makes 40% (5% defective). Find the probability a random part is defective, and — if it is — that it came from A.

P(def) = 0.60·0.02 + 0.40·0.05 = 0.012 + 0.020 = 0.032

P(A | def) = 0.012 / 0.032 = 0.375

Where Camila will trip
  • Adding when she should multiply, or vice versa — the "or" vs "and" mix-up.
  • Forgetting to subtract the overlap in an "or" problem.
  • Treating dependent draws as independent (not updating the counts after the first draw).
  • Flipping the conditional: confusing P(A | B) with P(B | A).
How to teach it

Give her two trigger words: "or" means add (then subtract the overlap), "and"/"then" means multiply. Make the complement trick her default for any "at least one" problem — it's almost always faster. For anything with multiple stages, draw the tree: branch probabilities multiply, separate paths add. For conditional probability, have her say it aloud — "given B happened, the world is now just B" — and use real objects (cards, marbles) so the counts are visible, not abstract.

Unit 6

Expected Value

Barnett §8.5 · expected value of a random variable
Foundations
  • Weighted average: multiply each value by its weight, then add the products.
  • Σ (sigma) means "sum all the terms."
  • The weights here are probabilities — they must cover all outcomes and sum to 1.

The method

Expected value is a probability-weighted average: E(X) = Σ xᵢ · P(xᵢ). The reliable way to compute it is a three-column table — outcomes, their probabilities, and the product of the two — then sum the product column. It represents the long-run average per trial. A game is fair when E = 0; decisions compare expected values across options. Two things to watch: losses are negative values, and the probabilities must account for every outcome (they sum to 1).

E(X) = Σ xᵢ · P(xᵢ) · fair game ⇔ E(X) = 0

Worked examples

Example 1 · the table method

A raffle sells 500 tickets. Prizes: one $200 and three $50. Find the expected winnings of a single ticket.

E = (1/500)(200) + (3/500)(50) + (496/500)(0)

= (200 + 150)/500 = 350/500 = $0.70. If the ticket costs $2, the expected net is 0.70 − 2 = −$1.30.

Example 2 · a simple game

Roll a die: win $6 if you roll a 6, otherwise lose $1. Find the expected value.

E = (1/6)(6) + (5/6)(−1) = 1 − 5/6 = 1/6 ≈ +$0.17 — slightly favorable to the player.

Where Camila will trip
  • Leaving out an outcome, so the probabilities don't sum to 1.
  • Sign errors — entering a loss as positive.
  • Confusing expected winnings with expected net (forgetting to subtract the cost to play).
How to teach it

Insist on the three-column table every time (outcome, probability, product) — the structure itself prevents most errors. Before multiplying, have her confirm the probability column sums to 1. Frame the result as "the average if you played this thousands of times," which makes a negative expected value concrete and intuitive. And explicitly separate the two questions she'll be asked: expected winnings versus expected net after the cost to play.

Unit 7

Markov Chains

Barnett §9.1–9.2 · transition matrices, state vectors, steady state
Foundations
  • Probability vector: nonnegative entries that sum to 1.
  • Row vector × matrix: [a b]·P combines the row entries with each column — a 1×n times n×n gives 1×n.
  • Matrix power Pⁿ = P multiplied by itself n times (n-step transitions).

The method

A system moves between states with fixed probabilities collected in a transition matrix P — each row sums to 1, and entry (i, j) is the probability of moving from state i to state j. The current situation is a state vector S (a row). Step forward by multiplying on the right: S₁ = S₀·P, and n steps out is S₀·Pⁿ. If the chain is regular, it settles into a unique steady-state vector π no matter where it began. You find π by solving πP = π together with the condition that its entries sum to 1 — for two states, that's just one small equation.

Worked examples

Example 1 · stepping forward

Weather: states Sunny and Rainy, with P = [[0.7, 0.3],[0.4, 0.6]] (row = today, column = tomorrow). It's sunny today, so S₀ = [1, 0].

Tomorrow: S₁ = [1·0.7 + 0·0.4,   1·0.3 + 0·0.6] = [0.7, 0.3]

Day after: S₂ = S₁·P = [0.7·0.7 + 0.3·0.4,   0.7·0.3 + 0.3·0.6] = [0.61, 0.39]

Example 2 · steady state

For the same P, find the long-run fraction of sunny days. Let π = [a, 1−a] and use the first component of πP = π:

0.7a + 0.4(1−a) = a → 0.3a + 0.4 = a → 0.4 = 0.7a → a = 4/7

So π = [4/7, 3/7] ≈ [0.571, 0.429]. Check: (4/7)(0.7)+(3/7)(0.4) = 4/7 ✓

Where Camila will trip
  • Multiplying matrix × vector instead of vector × matrix (wrong orientation).
  • Building a transition matrix whose rows don't sum to 1.
  • Forgetting the "sum to 1" equation when solving for the steady state.
  • Algebra slips in the steady-state equation.
How to teach it

Anchor the whole topic in a concrete story — weather, or customers switching between two coffee shops — so the matrix means something. Drill the row-vector-times-matrix multiplication slowly, by hand, several times, because the orientation is the part she'll get wrong. Then frame steady state as the natural question: "where does it settle in the long run, regardless of how it started?" The solve itself is just a small system of equations, so it ties straight back to Unit 2. Always finish by plugging π back into πP as a sanity check.

Unit 8

Bernoulli & Binomial

Barnett §10.4 · Bernoulli trials, the binomial distribution
Foundations
  • Combinations C(n,k) = ways to choose k of n, order ignored (Unit 4).
  • Exponents: pᵏ = p multiplied k times; p⁰ = 1.
  • Independent events multiply; complementary probabilities p and 1−p sum to 1.

The method

A Bernoulli trial is one success/failure experiment with a fixed probability p. A binomial setting is n of those: a fixed number of independent trials, the same p each time, counting total successes. The probability of exactly k successes is P(X = k) = C(n,k) · pᵏ · (1−p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ — three pieces: how many arrangements (the C), the successes (pᵏ), and the failures ((1−p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ). The expected number of successes is simply np, and for "at least one" it's almost always faster to use the complement, 1 − P(none).

P(X = k) = C(n,k) · pᵏ · (1−p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ · mean = n·p

Worked examples

Example 1 · exactly k successes

A free-throw shooter makes 70% (p = 0.7). In 5 attempts, find P(exactly 4 made).

C(5,4)·(0.7)⁴·(0.3)¹ = 5 · 0.2401 · 0.3 = 0.360

Example 2 · "at least one" via complement

A student guesses 4 true/false questions (p = 0.5). Find P(at least one correct).

1 − (0.5)⁴ = 1 − 0.0625 = 0.9375

Example 3 · mean plus a probability

Items are 5% defective (p = 0.05). In a box of 20, how many defects are expected, and what's P(none defective)?

Expected defects: np = 20 × 0.05 = 1

None defective: (0.95)²⁰ ≈ 0.358

Example 4 · exactly k, with dice

Roll a die 4 times. Find P(exactly two 6s), where p = 1/6.

C(4,2)·(1/6)²·(5/6)² = 6 · (1/36) · (25/36) = 150/1296 ≈ 0.116

Where Camila will trip
  • Dropping the C(n,k) factor — computing just pᵏ and forgetting the arrangements.
  • Wrong exponents: it's k successes and n−k failures.
  • Using the binomial when the trials aren't independent or p changes between them.
  • Summing many cases for "at least one" instead of using the complement.
How to teach it

Have her name the three pieces out loud every time: "how many ways (the C), the success power, the failure power." Make the complement the automatic move for "at least one." The mean np is the intuitive anchor — "70% of 5 shots is about 3.5 made," so getting 4 is plausible. To make the distribution feel real, build a small table of P(X = 0), P(X = 1), P(X = 2)… and notice it adds to 1.

The one thread that ties it together

Three of the eight units — matrices (2), linear programming (3), and Markov chains (7) — all come down to solving systems of equations by hand. If you get Camila genuinely fluent at that one skill early, those three units stop feeling like separate mountains and start feeling like the same move in different clothes. It's the highest-leverage thing to drill.
